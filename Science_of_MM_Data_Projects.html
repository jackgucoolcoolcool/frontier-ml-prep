PROJECT A · MixOracle — a predictive mixture optimizer for native-MM pretraining

Pretraining~9 months, team of 5

A1 · Problem & my role

Problem. The pretraining mixture for the next native-multimodal run (~40 sources, 8 families: web pairs, re-captioned pairs, interleaved docs, OCR/doc, grounding, video, audio, code+figures) was hand-tuned by committee. Nobody could answer "what happens to ChartQA if we halve interleaved and double OCR?" without a frontier-scale gamble. The org wanted the recipe to be a prediction, not a negotiation.

My role (be specific — interviewers probe this): I was the technical lead for the mixture-modeling workstream (1 of 3 workstreams; ~5 people total on the project). Personally: I designed the proxy ladder and its calibration gate, derived and implemented the capacity-aware mixing-law fit, owned the constrained optimizer, and wrote the final recipe recommendation doc that the pretraining lead signed off on. I did not own the data pipelines (partner team) or the eval harness (I consumed it, and later drove one major change to it — see A3-P2). Two research engineers ran the proxy sweeps; I designed every sweep and analyzed every result.

A2 · Methodology — iterated honestly (v1 → v2 → v3)

v1 — "DoReMi it" (failed usefully, ~6 weeks)

First attempt was the obvious one: run Group-DRO reweighting with a 300M proxy à la DoReMi. It "worked" — produced weights — but two things killed it: (1) DRO optimizes worst-case excess log-loss per domain, which for MM data pathologically upweighted the noisiest OCR shards (high reducible loss ≠ high capability value); (2) the weights were a black box — no way to answer counterfactuals ("what if OCR cap doubles next quarter?") without re-running. Lesson I carried forward: I didn't want a weight-producer, I wanted a differentiable model of the recipe space.

v2 — explicit mixing laws (the workhorse)

Switched to the Data Mixing Laws paradigm: ~60 proxy runs at Latin-hypercube-sampled weights, fit per-eval exponential laws, optimize analytically:

Ŝ_e(w) = c_e + a_e · exp( Σ_i  b_{e,i} · w_i )        # per-eval e, per-family i
w* = argmax_w Σ_e λ_e Ŝ_e(w)   s.t.  Σw=1,  w_i ≤ cap_i,  Ŝ_text(w) ≥ floor

Held-out mixture R² hit 0.93 at 1B scale. Then the 3B check exposed rank inversion on reasoning-heavy evals — the 1B-optimal mix underweighted dense/interleaved data that only pays off with capacity (exactly the failure mode the Capacity-Aware Mixture Law paper later formalized).

v3 — capacity-aware laws + rank-fidelity gating (what shipped)

  • Made N (params) an explicit axis: refit jointly over (w, N) at 300M/1B/3B so b-coefficients could flip sign with scale — some did (interleaved docs: mildly negative at 300M, strongly positive at 3B).
  • Calibration gate: no proxy rung enters the fit unless its eval ranking over a fixed probe set of 12 mixtures achieves Spearman ρ ≥ 0.8 against the 8B reference. The 300M rung failed for reasoning evals and was excluded for those evals only — per-eval proxy trust, not global.
  • Video handled off-model: Phase-1 dose–response showed video eval-per-token was dominated by tokens-per-clip, not clip count (consistent with EVS/GIFT temporal-redundancy results). So video entered the optimizer as a fixed, separately-optimized token-efficiency policy (aggressive keyframe sampling), not a free weight — reducing the dimensionality the law had to explain.
  • Extrapolate → verify: solved w* at target N, one frontier run, prediction residuals tracked as the pipeline's own quality metric.

A3 · Real problems I faced (beyond the v1/v2 pivots)

P1 · The mixing law broke exactly where it mattered: near the caps ▶

Symptom. Fit quality was great in the interior of the simplex but the optimizer, naturally, pushed solutions to the boundary (max out OCR, max out re-captioned pairs) — where we had the fewest proxy samples and the exponential form is least trustworthy. Classic optimize-into-your-model's-blind-spot.

Fix. Two rounds of active sampling: after the first fit, I re-allocated ~15 proxy runs to the neighborhood of the proposed w* (trust-region style) and refit. Also added an epistemic penalty — the solve maximized Ŝ minus κ·(bootstrap std of Ŝ) so it stopped chasing regions the fit couldn't defend. The optimum moved noticeably (~30% relative shift in two families) after the trust-region runs; without them we'd have shipped a fit artifact.

P2 · Multi-epoch reality: laws fit on unique tokens, but the run would repeat data ▶

Symptom. Several high-value families (dense human-grade captions, grounding) were capped — at the target budget they'd repeat 4–8 epochs. My law treated w_i as sampling probability over infinite data; it had no concept of repetition decay, and repeated-data value decays hard after a few epochs.

Fix. Extended each family term with an effective-token discount: D_eff,i = U_i · (1 − r^{e_i})/(1 − r) (U = unique tokens, e = epochs, r ≈ repetition decay fit from dedicated 2-epoch/4-epoch proxy runs). This changed the answer materially: the optimizer stopped over-allocating to small gold sets and instead flagged their U_i as the binding constraint — which became the formal justification for funding Project B's data-generation engine. The two projects connected here.

P3 · The eval suite itself was the bottleneck (I had to fight for a suite change) ▶

Symptom. Our MM eval aggregate was dominated by near-saturated VQA-style benchmarks; mixture deltas moved them <0.5 pt while moving DocVQA/Chart/counting by 3–5 pts. Optimizing λ-weighted aggregate meant optimizing noise on saturated evals.

Fix. Proposed and got adopted a headroom-weighted objective: λ_e ∝ (ceiling_e − current_e) × strategic weight, recomputed each quarter. Politically delicate — saturated-benchmark owners read it as demotion. What won the argument was a variance decomposition showing that 70% of aggregate movement across proxy arms was seed noise from three saturated evals. Data science includes eval science; you can't fit laws against a broken objective.

P4 · A pipeline change silently invalidated half my sweeps ▶

Symptom. Mid-project, the (partner-owned) data pipeline shipped an improved OCR-quality filter. Great for the model, catastrophic for me: proxy runs before/after the change were trained on different distributions under the same source name — 25 of my 60 sweep points were no longer comparable.

Fix. Short-term: refit on post-change runs only, ate the precision loss. Structural: instituted recipe pinning — every source referenced by immutable snapshot ID + filter version; any pipeline change creates a new ID; the law's covariates are snapshot IDs, never source names. Boring data-engineering fix, but it's the single change that made the project reproducible. (This is the lineage lesson from the field-map doc, learned the hard way.)

A4 · Results & what I would claim out loud

  • Prediction: frontier-run eval vector landed within the bootstrap CI on 9 of 11 tracked capability clusters; the two misses (long-video, multi-image reasoning) were long-context-sensitive — flagged as a known limitation of short-context proxies, and turned into next-cycle work.
  • Iso-FLOP win: beat the incumbent hand-tuned mixture on the headroom-weighted aggregate with pure-text within guardrail (the fitted cross-terms located interference in one high-volume noisy-pair family; capping only it preserved text at near-zero MM cost).
  • Counterfactual capability: the standing artifact is that recipe questions ("what if source X doubles?") are now answered in minutes from the fitted law, with a confidence interval, instead of via a committee meeting — that's the cultural deliverable.

PROJECT B · CapForge — a re-captioning + quality-tier engine, delivered through mid-training anneal

Data engine + mid-training~6 months, team of 4, spun out of A3-P2

B1 · Problem & my role

Problem. Project A proved that unique high-density caption tokens were the binding constraint — the optimizer wanted more than existed. Meanwhile the literature converged on the answer: MetaCLIP-v2-style iterative re-captioning and Molmo/PixMo-style human dense captions establish that caption density/fidelity, not pair count, sets the perception ceiling. Charter: manufacture the missing gold-tier tokens, prove their value causally, and deliver them where they pay most — the mid-training anneal.

My role: I initiated and led this project (it was my A3-P2 finding that justified it). Personally: designed the captioner-improvement loop and its human-annotation spec, defined the quality-tier rubric and the tier-classifier training recipe, and ran the anneal-composition experiments end-to-end. A partner applied-team ran annotation ops; an infra engineer owned the batch-inference fleet. I made the two decisive calls documented in B2/B3 and presented the anneal recipe that shipped.

B2 · The engine — iterated (and re-scoped once, mid-flight)

v1 — captioner bootstrap loop

  1. Seed: collect a PixMo-style human dense-caption set — speech-first annotation (annotators talk for 60–90s per image; ASR + cleanup), because speaking produces ~200-word density that typing never does, and it's distillation-free.
  2. Train captioner v1 on the seed; caption 50M images; humans edit (not write) a sample of outputs — edit distance becomes both a quality metric and new training signal; iterate ~3 rounds (the MetaCLIP-v2 pattern: iterative human refinement of the captioner, not of the corpus).
  3. Tiering: a lightweight scorer (VLM-judge distilled into a small classifier, following the unified MM quality-classifier approach) buckets all caption data into T0 (human/edited), T1 (model, judge-passed), T2 (model, unverified), T3 (raw alt-text).

v2 — the re-scope: from "filter harder" to "mix smarter"

Original plan had a hard filtering stage — drop T3 aggressively. Two findings forced a re-scope: our own dose–response showed T3 still carried unique long-tail entity coverage (dropping it hurt rare-concept recognition), and DataComp-VLM's headline result landed at the same time: mixing, not filtering, is the dominant lever; quality filters can go negative. So v2 kept every tier and made tier ratio the optimized variable — including a MetaCLIP-v2-style ~15% synthetic-caption sampling rate against original alt-text for the same image (both texts kept, sampled stochastically, so the model sees clean and wild registers of the same visual content).

v3 — delivery via anneal, not base mix

Where to spend the gold tokens? The mid-training literature (OLMo-2, Llama-3, Phi-4; IBM's study) says high-quality data under fast LR decay yields the steepest gain per token. I ran a 2×2 at proxy scale: {gold in base mix vs gold in final-15% anneal} × {LR decay fast vs standard}. Anneal+fast-decay dominated — same tokens, ~2.3× the eval lift versus spreading them through the base mix. Final recipe: T0/T1 concentrated in the anneal window with a reserved general-data ratio to prevent backward regression; T2/T3 carry the base mix.

B3 · Real problems I faced

P1 · The judge and the captioner shared a lineage — self-preference corrupted the tiers ▶

Symptom. Tier-1 acceptance rates climbed each captioner round — looked like progress. A blinded human audit said quality was flat. Root cause: judge and captioner descended from the same base model; the judge increasingly preferred its sibling's style (fluent, confident, occasionally wrong). Self-preference bias, quietly re-labeling style as quality.

Fix. (1) Re-anchored the judge every round with fresh human-edit data, making human edit distance the calibration target rather than judge score. (2) Added adversarial probes to the judge's eval: captions with planted errors (wrong count, wrong color, invented object) that a sound judge must catch — judge ships only if planted-error recall stays > threshold. (3) Diversified: final tiering used judge score ∧ OCR-consistency check ∧ CLIP-style alignment score; disagreement routed to human audit. Acceptance-rate "progress" flattened to honest levels — the right outcome.

P2 · Captions drifted toward a house style — measurable diversity collapse ▶

Symptom. By round 3, model captions converged on a recognizable template ("The image shows… In the foreground… The overall mood…"). N-gram entropy of the caption corpus fell every round. Downstream, models trained on it produced eerily uniform descriptions and got worse at matching the register of user prompts.

Fix. Style conditioning: the captioner was retrained to produce k registers per image (terse inventory / dense spatial / OCR-focused / narrative), with register sampled at training time — turning one image into diverse-text supervision instead of one house-style paragraph. Also kept the 15% synthetic / 85%-of-the-time-original alt-text sampling precisely because wild text is a diversity reservoir. Tracked corpus n-gram entropy and embedding dispersion as first-class release metrics for every CapForge drop.

P3 · Anneal gains that didn't survive post-training ▶

Symptom. The anneal delivered clean base-model gains (captioning, DocVQA, counting probes). After the standard SFT+RL pipeline, half the delta vanished on chat-style evals. Panic moment: did the anneal only teach a style that SFT overwrote?

Fix. Diagnosis first: built probe evals separating perception (does the model latently see it? — likelihood-based and forced-choice probes) from expression (does it say it in chat format?). Result: perception gains survived post-training fully; expression gains were being overwritten by SFT data that was less dense than our anneal data. The fix was downstream: regenerated a slice of SFT responses to inherit CapForge density (per A-doc logic: post-training should elicit what pretraining installed, and our SFT was actively suppressing it). Recovered most of the delta. Lesson I'd state crisply: an anneal gain isn't real until it survives your own post-training pipeline — measure through the full stack.

P4 · 50M-image batch inference was the actual bottleneck, and quality-vs-cost had to be decided ▶

Symptom. Re-captioning at corpus scale with the best captioner was compute-comparable to a small pretraining run. Finance asked the fair question: is a caption from the 8× cheaper distilled captioner 8× worse?

Fix. Ran it as a science question, same machinery as everything else: dose–response of downstream eval vs captioner-quality-tier at fixed token count. Found a clean knee — the mid-size distilled captioner retained ~90% of downstream value at ~15% of cost, except on OCR-heavy and fine-grained images, where the big model was irreplaceable. Shipped a router: cheap captioner by default; big captioner for images flagged OCR-dense/fine-grained by a trivial pre-classifier. ~70% cost cut at ~97% of the downstream value. This is the kind of concrete, quantified engineering-meets-science tradeoff interviewers love.

B4 · Results & the laws it left behind

  • Causal value proof: matched-token ablation (CapForge anneal vs continued base mix) showed unambiguous CI-clearing gains concentrated exactly where Project A's law predicted headroom (doc/chart/counting/dense description) — the two projects cross-validated each other.
  • Durable curves: tier-ratio dose–response ("how much T0 is enough before saturation"), the anneal-vs-base-mix 2.3× token-efficiency factor, and the captioner-quality/cost knee — three reusable laws, each answering a budget question.
  • Process artifacts: the judge-anchoring protocol (planted-error recall gate) and diversity release-metrics became standing requirements for any synthetic data source org-wide.

"Only doers know" — the trench details that make the narrative unmistakably real

These are the details no paper reports and no reader of papers would invent — the texture of actually running these projects. Drop one or two into an answer and the interviewer's posture changes. Grouped by where they'd come up.

T1 · Dataloader truths (Project A)

  • Nominal vs realized mixture. We set weights as document-sampling probabilities, but an interleaved doc is ~100× the tokens of an alt-text pair, packing splits images across sequence boundaries, and loss-masking (no loss on image tokens in some streams) changes which tokens produce gradient. Our first audit found realized gradient-token share off from nominal w by up to 2.3× for two families. Every sweep before the audit was refit on realized shares logged from the dataloader itself. I now consider "do you fit on nominal or realized tokens?" the single fastest tell of whether a mixture paper is trustworthy.
  • Determinism as a science requirement. Variance-reduction across sweep arms (same data order, same seeds) requires the loader to be bit-deterministic under resumption — and ours wasn't: a checkpoint-resume reshuffled shard order, so any arm that hit a preemption saw different data. Preemptions are random ⇒ silent unblinded noise injected into exactly the arms that ran longest. Fixing loader determinism cut our measured arm-to-arm σ by ~30%. Nobody writes this in papers; everybody who has done sweeps on shared clusters knows it.
  • The 4-tile bug. For weeks, portrait-orientation images were getting their thumbnail tile dropped by an aspect-ratio edge case — invisible in loss (tiny fraction of tokens), visible as a persistent, inexplicable deficit on document evals for portrait scans. Found it by binning eval errors by image aspect ratio, which is now a standing slice in our eval reports. Lesson: when a capability underperforms its law prediction, bin the errors by every metadata field you have before touching the mixture.

T2 · Fitting-room truths (Project A)

  • Which loss do you even read? A WSD decay branch ends at a checkpoint; val loss at that checkpoint still carries ~0.5σ of batch-composition jitter. We read an EMA of the last ~2% of steps, measured in fp32 regardless of training dtype (bf16 val loss has a visible quantization floor at small models). Sounds pedantic; at 150M–300M scale this jitter was the difference between a clean fit and a mushy one.
  • Loss spikes are data forensics. Every spike got a shard-level autopsy: the top-3 causes were (1) a shard of near-blank PDF pages with dense OCR labels (label noise), (2) mega-resolution panoramas exploding tile count, (3) one batch of corrupted JPEGs decoding to noise. We kept a "spike gallery" — literally a slide deck of the offending images — and it did more for stakeholder trust in the pipeline than any R² number, because leadership could see what quality filtering means.
  • The fit you don't ship. For every headline fit there were ~5 fits that died in the notebook: exponents pinned at bounds (unidentified), bootstrap CIs spanning sign flips, an α that only looked stable because two runs dominated the Huber loss. The discipline that saved us: every coefficient ships with its bootstrap histogram attached, and any reviewer can veto on the histogram alone. Point estimates presented naked are how scaling-law theater happens.

T3 · Data-engine truths (Project B)

  • ASR artifacts became model tics. The speech-first captions carried transcription quirks — "um", false starts, spoken-number formats ("twenty twenty-three" for 2023), and dictation punctuation. Round-1 captioner faithfully learned them; downstream models occasionally described images with spoken-register numbers. The cleanup pass (a small text-normalization model over ASR output) mattered as much as the annotation protocol itself. Human-data pipelines have their own synthetic artifacts — "human" is not the same as "clean."
  • Annotator throughput has a quality curve too. Edit-based QA (humans fix model captions) drifted over weeks: as the captioner improved, annotators' edit rate fell — partly real, partly learned laziness (rubber-stamping). We caught it with seeded honeypots: known-flawed captions injected at ~3% rate; an annotator's honeypot catch-rate gates their throughput bonus. Catch-rates jumped 20 points the week after we told people honeypots existed. Data quality is a mechanism-design problem before it's an ML problem.
  • The judge prompt is a hyperparameter with a changelog. A one-line edit to the judge's rubric prompt ("penalize omissions" → "penalize omissions of salient objects") moved tier boundaries enough to shift the T1/T2 split by 8% of the corpus — mid-project, which would have confounded every downstream dose–response. Judge prompts went under version control with the same recipe-pinning discipline as data snapshots, and tier IDs record the judge version that produced them.
  • Batch-inference economics live in the tail. The re-captioning fleet's cost wasn't set by mean image but by the tail: mega-resolution images and 100-object scenes that blew generation length. Two mundane interventions — a hard cap on caption tokens with graceful truncation, and routing the top-1% resolution tail to a downscaled pass — cut fleet cost ~25% with unmeasurable quality impact. "Only doers know" because only the person paying the fleet bill ever looks at the length histogram.

T4 · Organizational truths (both projects)

  • The graph that actually convinced leadership wasn't the law fit — it was a two-panel plot: predicted-vs-actual eval for the frontier run (with CI bands), next to the same plot for the previous, vibes-based recipe cycle (no prediction existed, so the panel was empty). The empty panel did the persuading. Communicating counterfactual discipline beats communicating fit quality.
  • Sweeps are a political currency. Proxy-compute allocation was contested weekly; the thing that protected the run matrix was publishing, in advance, what each block would decide (not what it would measure). A block tied to a decision survives budget review; a block tied to a curiosity doesn't. I learned to write "this 15-run block chooses between recipe A and B for the frontier run" instead of "this block characterizes repetition decay."
  • Deprecation is a data decision. The hardest single call of Project B was deleting the round-1 caption corpus (50M captions, real money) after round-3 supervision superseded it — because keeping it in the mix measurably dragged style diversity, but the sunk cost made deletion organizationally radioactive. We shipped it as an experiment ("round-1 in vs out at iso-token"), let the paired delta make the call, and archived rather than deleted. Frame destructive decisions as reversible experiments and they become makeable.
Why this section wins interviews: papers establish that you know the field; trench details establish that you've lived in it. The realized-token audit (T1), the bootstrap-histogram veto (T2), the honeypot mechanism (T3), and the decision-tied sweep blocks (T4) are the four I'd weave in unprompted — one per answer, never more.

STAR one-pagers — say-it-out-loud versions (~90 seconds each)

Project A in 90 seconds

"Our multimodal pretraining mixture was set by committee, and nobody could predict what a recipe change would do without a frontier-scale gamble. I led the mixture-modeling workstream: we fit capacity-aware data-mixing laws on a ladder of proxy models — with a hard gate that a proxy rung is only trusted for evals where it rank-correlates with a larger reference — then solved a constrained optimization with a text-regression floor. The interesting failures: the optimizer chasing the fit's blind spots at the simplex boundary, which we fixed with trust-region active sampling; and repetition — small gold datasets get epoch-ed, so I added an effective-token discount, which revealed that unique high-quality caption tokens were the binding constraint. The frontier run confirmed the prediction on 9 of 11 capability clusters and beat the hand-tuned recipe at iso-FLOP. And the binding-constraint finding directly justified my second project."

Project B in 90 seconds

"Project A said the constraint was unique dense-caption tokens, so I started CapForge to manufacture them: a PixMo-style speech-first human seed set, a MetaCLIP-style iteratively human-refined captioner, and quality tiers. Two pivots mattered. First, we planned to filter aggressively and instead learned — consistent with DataComp-VLM — that mixing beats filtering: raw alt-text carries irreplaceable long-tail coverage, so tiers became mixture variables, not gates. Second, delivery: a 2×2 showed the same gold tokens were ~2.3× more valuable concentrated in a fast-LR-decay mid-training anneal than spread through the base mix. Hardest problem was measurement integrity — our judge shared lineage with the captioner and developed self-preference, which we caught with blinded human audits and fixed by anchoring the judge to human edit distance with planted-error recall gates. And gains had to survive the full post-training stack — we found SFT was overwriting expression gains and fixed the SFT data, not the anneal."

Cross-exam Q&A — the follow-ups a Gemini interviewer would actually ask

"Why exponential mixing laws and not just a neural predictor over recipes?" ▶

Sample efficiency and extrapolation honesty. With ~60 runs, a low-parameter form with the right inductive bias (diminishing returns per family) fits tightly and its failure modes are legible — you can see when a coefficient is unidentified. A neural predictor with 60 points memorizes the interior and extrapolates silently and confidently wrong, exactly where the optimizer goes. I'd revisit with 500+ runs; CLIMB-style predictor-guided iterative search is the natural upgrade path.

"Your calibration gate needs an 8B reference — isn't that circular / expensive?" ▶

It's one-time capital, amortized: a single 8B reference model plus ~12 probe mixtures at 8B-short-budget calibrates the entire proxy ladder for the whole project. The alternative — trusting uncalibrated proxies — cost us six weeks in v2 when rank inversion surfaced. And it's not circular: the reference validates ranking fidelity of cheap runs, it doesn't set the weights.

"How do you know CapForge captions don't just teach benchmark style?" ▶

Three defenses: image-space decontamination against all eval images before captioning; the perception-vs-expression probe split from B3-P3 (likelihood/forced-choice probes are format-free, so gains there can't be style artifacts); and transfer checks on evals from families never targeted by the caption distribution. The perception probes surviving post-training was the strongest evidence the gain was representational, not stylistic.

"If you could only keep one result from both projects?" ▶

The effective-token discount + binding-constraint analysis (A3-P2). It's the piece that converts "more data is better" into an actual allocation theory: it tells you when to stop buying volume and start buying uniqueness, it priced the value of a data-generation engine before we built it, and it's the bridge between scaling-law thinking and data-engine investment — which is, I think, the core open question of the science-of-data field right now.

"What would you do next?" ▶

Close the loop the two misses exposed: long-context-sensitive capabilities (long-video, multi-image) don't proxy well at short context — I'd build a context-length-aware rung of the proxy ladder. Then the bigger swing: extend mixing laws from pretraining into a joint PT+anneal+SFT allocation law — B3-P3 proved the stages interact (SFT can overwrite anneal gains), so optimizing them independently is leaving capability on the table.
