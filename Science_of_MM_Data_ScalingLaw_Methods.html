How I Developed the Scaling Laws — Data (esp. MM), Model Size & Learning Rate

The deepest layer of the MixOracle narrative (Project A in Science_of_MM_Data_Projects.html): the actual mechanics. Functional forms and why, the three fitting methods and which I trusted, the LR/batch laws you must nail first or every data law is confounded, the multimodal joint law, the run matrix, the fitting code, the validation protocol, and the confounds.

Framing: first-person Gemini rehearsal narrative, technically faithful to Kaplan/Chinchilla/Muennighoff/μP/WSD/Apple-NMM public literature. This is the doc for when the interviewer says "okay, but how exactly did you fit it?"

§1 · What "developing a scaling law" operationally means

A scaling law project is four artifacts, in dependency order. Skipping a layer is how people produce laws that don't extrapolate:

  1. A tuning law — how optimal LR, batch size, and schedule move with N and D. Without this, every larger model in your sweep is differently mistuned, and "the scaling law" is partly a law of your mistuning.
  2. A base compute law — L(N, D) on a fixed data distribution. This calibrates the machinery (fit quality, extrapolation error) on the easiest problem.
  3. A data law — extend D from "token count" to "token count × composition × quality × repetition."
  4. The MM joint law — per-modality token budgets and mixture weights as first-class variables, with interaction terms.
The order is the method. I spent the first ~2 months on layers 1–2 with zero multimodal content. That felt slow to stakeholders; it's what made layers 3–4 fast and trustworthy. Every scaling-law disaster story I know is someone fitting layer 4 with layer 1 broken.

§2 · The base law: L(N, D) and the three ways to fit it

2.1 The functional form and what each piece means

L(N, D) = E  +  A / N^α  +  B / D^β          (Chinchilla form)

E : irreducible loss — entropy of the data distribution itself.
    CRUCIAL in MM: E differs per modality stream (image-conditioned text
    has lower entropy than free text; OCR tokens lower still).
A/N^α : capacity-limited term — what a finite model can't represent.
B/D^β : data-limited term — what finite data can't teach.
Compute-optimal: minimize L s.t. C ≈ 6ND  →  N* ∝ C^a, D* ∝ C^b,
    a = β/(α+β),  b = α/(α+β).   Chinchilla found a ≈ b ≈ 0.5.

2.2 The three estimation methods — and which I trusted

MethodHowProsCons / when it lies
M1 · Fixed-N training curvesTrain each N once, long; read L(D) along the curveCheapest — one run gives a whole D-sliceIntermediate points are off-schedule (LR not decayed for that budget) → systematically pessimistic mid-curve; Kaplan's undertrained-optima error came from exactly this
M2 · IsoFLOP profilesFor each compute level C, train several (N, D=C/6N) pairs each fully tuned to its budget; fit the parabola in log N; the minimum is (N*, D*) at that CEvery point on-schedule; minima are model-free readings; robustExpensive — every point is a separate full run
M3 · Parametric joint fitFit E, A, B, α, β on all (N, D, L) runs at onceUses everything; gives the full surface, not just optimaFit is delicate (see §7); form misspecification hides in nice-looking R²

My protocol: M2 as ground truth at 4 compute levels; M3 fit on all runs; consistency between M3's implied optima and M2's read-off minima was the acceptance test for the functional form. First pass failed it — traced to the LR confound (§3) — which is precisely the kind of failure M3 alone would have absorbed silently into biased exponents.

Bookkeeping details that change exponents (interview gold): (1) count non-embedding N or the small-model end bends the fit — with a large multimodal vocab + image tokenizer, embedding params are a bigger fraction than in text-only, so this bites harder; (2) C≈6ND ignores attention FLOPs — at long context (video!) use the exact FLOP count or D is mismeasured; (3) evaluate L on a fixed held-out set with a frozen tokenizer across the entire sweep — any tokenizer change mid-project silently re-scales D and L at once.

§3 · The LR & batch laws — the layer everyone skips, and why WSD changed the economics

3.1 Why this comes first

Optimal LR falls with model size; optimal batch rises with compute. If your sweep uses one LR recipe everywhere, big models are undertuned → their loss is inflated → α is biased low → the fitted law says "params help less than they do" → you allocate wrongly at the frontier. The tuning law isn't a hyperparameter chore; it's a correctness precondition of the scaling law.

3.2 What I used

  • μP (maximal update parametrization) as the backbone. Reparametrize init scale, per-layer LR multipliers, and output scaling so the optimal base LR is approximately width-invariant. Tune once on a small proxy, transfer across width. I validated the transfer claim empirically — LR sweeps at 3 widths — rather than assuming it: μP held well for the text tower, but the vision patch-embedding path needed its own multiplier (its input statistics don't follow the width-scaling assumptions μP makes for hidden layers). One extra tuned constant; without it, larger models were consistently mistuned on image tokens and the image-loss exponent was biased.
  • Residual depth/LR fit. μP handles width; depth and D still shift the optimum mildly. Fit a small power law η*(N, D) = η₀ · N^{-γ_N} · D^{γ_D} on top of μP from cheap LR sweeps (γ's were small but nonzero — consistent with the "μP plus residual corrections" experience most labs report).
  • Batch size via critical-noise-scale logic: below the gradient-noise scale, batch is a free parallelism knob; above it, you pay in tokens. Measured the noise scale at 3 points; kept every sweep run comfortably below critical batch so batch never entered the law as a confound.

3.3 WSD: the schedule trick that made the whole run matrix affordable

Cosine problem: LR schedule must END at the token budget for the loss to be
"on-schedule". One cosine run of length D gives ONE valid (N,D,L) point —
mid-curve readings are off-schedule and biased.

WSD (Warmup–Stable–Decay):
  warmup → long STABLE plateau at η_max → short DECAY (~10–20%) to ~0.
  From ONE stable trunk, branch a cheap decay at D₁, D₂, D₃, ...
  → one run yields MANY on-schedule (N, Dᵢ, L) points at ~15% extra cost each.

This is the single biggest lever on sweep economics: my run matrix (§6) would have been ~4× more expensive under cosine. It also composes perfectly with data work: the decay branch is exactly where the anneal/mid-training mixture goes — so the same branching infrastructure that gave me L(D) points also gave me the anneal-composition experiments of Project B (CapForge). One infra investment, two projects served.

WSD caveat I hit: loss at the branch point (stable phase, high LR) is not comparable to decayed loss — the decay drop is large and itself mildly N-dependent. Only decayed-branch endpoints enter the fit; never mix trunk readings into the law.

§4 · From "tokens" to "data": repetition and quality enter the law

4.1 Repetition (data-constrained scaling, Muennighoff-style)

Replace raw D with effective data:
  D_eff = U · (1 − r^e) / (1 − r)        U = unique tokens, e = epochs
  L(N, D) → L(N, D_eff)                  r ≈ decay per epoch (fit ≈ 0.5–0.7)
Empirically: ~4 epochs is near-free, value ≈ 0 past ~15–40 epochs.
MM twist: r is FAMILY-SPECIFIC. Dense captions tolerated more epochs than
noisy alt-text (less to memorize per token ⇒ slower value decay); grounding
data decayed fastest (format memorization). Fit r per family from paired
1/2/4/8-epoch branch runs off the same WSD trunk.

4.2 Quality as an effective-token multiplier

The cleanest way quality enters a law without exploding the parameter count: a per-tier multiplier q_t such that a token of tier t is worth q_t reference tokens: D_eff = Σ_t q_t · D_t (with repetition applied per tier). Fit q_t from tier-swap runs at fixed budget. Two honest findings: (1) q is eval-dependent — gold captions were worth ~3× on perception evals, ~1× on pure-text — so q_t is a vector, not a scalar, and the scalar-quality story you often hear is a lie of averaging; (2) q is mildly N-dependent (bigger models extract more from hard/dense data), which connects directly to the capacity-aware mixture-law finding in Project A.

§5 · The multimodal joint law — the centerpiece

5.1 Design decisions before any math

Decision 1 · What is "loss" in MM?

You cannot sum losses across modalities: image-conditioned-text, free-text, and (if trained generatively) image tokens have different entropy floors E. I kept a vector of per-stream validation losses — L_txt, L_img→txt, L_interleave, L_ocr, L_vid→txt — each on a frozen held-out set, and fit laws per stream. A single "MM loss" is a category error; this decision shaped everything downstream.

Decision 2 · What is "D" for an image?

Images have no God-given token count — it's a choice (resolution/tiling policy). I fixed one canonical tokenization for the whole sweep and treated resolution policy as a separate, later experiment. Mixing resolution changes into the sweep would have made D_img meaningless. (The resolution law was its own follow-up: loss vs tokens-per-image has its own saturating curve per task family.)

5.2 The functional form

For each validation stream s, with per-family token budgets D_i and weights w:

  L_s(N, D, w) = E_s + A_s/N^{α_s} + Σ_i  B_{s,i} / (q_i · D_eff,i)^{β_{s,i}}
                 + Σ_{i≠j} γ_{s,ij} · f(w_i, w_j)          ← interaction terms

Key empirical structure that made this fittable:
• α_s differed by stream (image-conditioned streams had SMALLER α — vision
  capability was more data-limited than capacity-limited in our regime).
• Most cross-terms γ ≈ 0. Interference was SPARSE: a few (noisy-pairs × text)
  and (video × interleaved) terms mattered; everything else dropped by
  regularization. Sparsity is what kept the parameter count sane.
• Transfer showed up as SHARED variance: text-math data measurably lowered
  chart-stream loss — a positive cross-term, the quantified version of
  "cross-modal transfer."

5.3 Two structural findings the law surfaced (worth stating as results)

  • Near-proportional N–D scaling held for the MM model (echoing Apple's NMM result that native multimodal follows LLM-like laws, params-and-tokens roughly in lockstep) — so the text-derived compute split was a fine prior, and the interesting deviations were all in the composition of D, not its total.
  • The compute-optimal mixture moves with scale. Because β and q vary by family and q grows with N for dense data, w* is a function of C: noisy web pairs' share should fall and dense/interleaved share rise along the compute frontier. A static mixture is optimal at exactly one scale — this became the headline chart of the project, and it is the formal justification for curriculum/anneal (early tokens cheap-and-broad, late tokens dense-and-hard follows from the same fitted surface).

§7 · Fitting code & validation protocol (where laws are made or faked)

7.1 The fit itself

# Chinchilla-style robust fit — the details that matter:
# 1. Fit in LOG space: predict log L via log-sum-exp of the terms
#    (heteroscedasticity: absolute loss errors shrink as loss falls).
# 2. Huber loss (δ ≈ measured seed-noise floor from T1 3-seed runs)
#    — a few divergent/lucky runs otherwise own the exponents.
# 3. L-BFGS from a COARSE GRID of inits over (α, β, log A, log B, log E)
#    — the landscape is multimodal; single-init fits find pretty wrong optima.
# 4. Bootstrap over RUNS (not tokens) → CI on every exponent and on w*.
#    Report w* as a REGION, not a point.
loss(θ) = Σ_runs huber_δ( logsumexp_model(θ; N,D_eff,w) − log L_obs )
# 5. For the MM law: group-lasso on cross-terms γ → sparse interference.

7.2 Validation — the part I'd lead with in an interview

  1. Extrapolation holdout, not interpolation R². Fit on runs ≤1B; predict the 3B and 8B holdouts; report prediction error in loss units and in "effective compute" units ("our 8B prediction was off by the equivalent of 8% compute"). Interpolation R² of 0.99 is decoration; extrapolation error is the product.
  2. Method cross-check: M3's implied frontier vs M2's read-off minima (§2.2). Disagreement = form misspecification, full stop.
  3. Residual autopsy: plot residuals vs N, D, w, context length, and launch date. Two real catches: a residual trend vs launch date exposed the silent data-pipeline change (Project A, P4 — recipe pinning); a residual trend vs context length flagged that long-context-sensitive streams weren't proxying (the two frontier misses we correctly anticipated).
  4. Decision-level validation: the law's job is choices, not curves — so the final test was rank agreement on held-out mixture pairs: does the law order T4 mixtures the way reality does? (It went 9/10.)

7.3 Bench notes — the measurement details only fitters know

  • What number is "the loss" of a run? EMA over the final ~2% of decayed-branch steps, val computed in fp32 (bf16 val shows a quantization floor at 150M–300M), on the frozen battery, excluding the first 3% of each val shard (cache-warm artifacts in our harness). Each clause exists because omitting it once produced a visibly worse fit.
  • Warmup exclusion & short-run bias. Points with D < ~20× warmup tokens were dropped: warmup is a fixed token tax, so short runs are systematically pessimistic and bend β upward. Cheap runs are exactly the ones a budget-pressed sweep over-produces — the bias is anti-correlated with your incentives, which is why it must be a hard rule, not a judgment call.
  • Realized-token bookkeeping in the fit. D and per-family D_i come from dataloader-logged, loss-masked gradient-token counts, never from the recipe's nominal weights (nominal-vs-realized diverged up to 2.3× — see the Projects doc trench section). The fitter ingests run manifests, not config files.
  • Curvature honesty. Residuals-vs-log-C showed mild systematic curvature at the smallest scales (consistent with broken-power-law behavior others report). Rather than upgrade to a 7-parameter broken form (unidentifiable on 160 runs), we down-weighted the sub-300M points for frontier extrapolation and documented the choice. Knowing when not to add parameters is most of applied curve fitting.
  • The two-run tyranny check. After every fit: recompute with each run left out (jackknife); any coefficient that moves >1σ under single-run deletion is flagged as "owned by one run" and the sweep gets a confirmatory point there. Twice this caught exponents that were artifacts of a single lucky 3B run.

7.4 Extrapolation range — can a fit at ≤5T tokens predict a 100T run?

Yes — predicting runs you can't afford is the entire job description of a scaling law, and 20× in tokens (1.3 orders of magnitude) is modest by precedent. GPT-4's final loss was predicted from runs with ≤1/10,000th the compute; Llama 3's 405B-on-15T operating point was chosen off small-run laws. But the extrapolation is only as good as four assumptions, and each one has a specific failure mode I'd name out loud:

The four validity conditions for 5T → 100T:
  1. Stationary data, no repetition. The power law assumes fresh samples from a fixed distribution. At 100T you will either repeat data or shift the mixture (high-quality sources exhaust first) — both silently bend β. If repetition is coming, fit the data-constrained form up front: D_eff = U·(1−r^e)/(1−r) per family (§4.1, §9) — repeated epochs are ≈free through ~4, ≈worthless past ~15 (Muennighoff).
  2. Say what's being scaled. At fixed N, L(D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β decays toward the model-capacity floor E + A/N^α — so a fixed-N 100T prediction mostly quantifies how little you'll gain. The interesting extrapolation is joint (N, D) along the compute frontier, which needs the full joint fit, not a D-slice.
  3. Exponent error compounds as R^δ — and the floor covariance dominates. See the arithmetic below; the killer isn't β noise, it's the E↔β identifiability trap (C4): two fits indistinguishable at ≤5T diverge at 100T. Buy identifiability with long-D branches at small N before trusting the fan.
  4. Predict loss with schedule-matched points; map to evals via the link. Every fit point needs an LR schedule matched to its horizon (cosine-matched or WSD decayed branches, §3.3) — intermediate checkpoints of one long cosine run are not valid points. And extrapolate in loss space, then convert through the fitted loss→eval link (C2); never fit laws on benchmark scores directly.
# Error propagation for a 20× extrapolation (R = D_target/D_fit_max = 20):
#   data term prediction:  B/D^(β+δ) vs B/D^β  →  relative error = R^δ
#   δ = 0.02 (a *good* fit's exponent CI)  →  20^0.02 = e^{0.02·ln20} ≈ 1.06   (6% on the D-term)
# Sounds fine — but at 100T the D-term is small vs E, so the PREDICTED LOSS
# is dominated by the fitted floor E, and E's CI is set by the E↔β covariance,
# not by R. The fan below is mostly floor uncertainty, not exponent noise.
# Rule of thumb: fit window ≥2 OoM of D (50B–5T), never points clustered at 5T;
# validate with one held-out pilot (10–20T) and report error in effective-compute units.

The speakable summary: "Twenty-x in tokens is a routine extrapolation for loss — GPT-4 did three-plus orders in compute — provided the 100T data is as fresh and as distributed as the 5T fit data, every fit point is schedule-matched, and the floor is identifiable. What I'd never promise off a 5T fit is a benchmark score at 100T: loss extrapolates, links can saturate, and repetition or mixture drift shows up exactly in the regime you didn't measure."

§8 · The confounds that nearly sank it (the honest war stories)

C1 · Optimal LR shifted with the mixture — tuning and data laws are coupled ▶

Symptom. Image-heavy mixtures had measurably different optimal LR than text-heavy ones at the same N (different gradient-noise statistics per stream). My clean separation "tune first, then sweep data" was leaky: mixtures far from the reference were mildly mistuned, and the mixing-law coefficients absorbed the mistuning — the fitted "value of image data" partly measured "how mistuned image-heavy runs were."

Fix. Measured ∂η*/∂w with cheap LR re-sweeps at 6 extreme mixture corners; found a simple correction (η* shifted with the image-token fraction). Re-ran the worst-affected sweep arms with corrected LR (~10 runs), refit — coefficient shifts up to 20% on the most affected families. This is my go-to war story for why the LR law is part of the data law.

C2 · Loss told a different story than evals — which does the law predict? ▶

Symptom. Per-stream loss was beautifully smooth and law-like; several capability evals (counting, OCR-exact-match) moved in jumps. Fitting laws directly on eval scores was noisy and form-less; fitting on loss risked optimizing something nobody ships.

Fix. Two-stage mapping: laws live in loss space (smooth, cheap, extrapolable); a per-eval sigmoidal loss→eval link function, fit once across all runs, converts predictions. The link is far more stable than eval-vs-(N,D,w) directly because loss is a sufficient statistic for most of the variation. Emergence-looking evals were mostly sharp-but-fixed links over smooth loss — so predicting loss + link anticipated most "jumps." The two that stayed unpredictable were flagged as genuine risk, not hidden.

C3 · Per-stream losses weren't comparable across mixtures — a subtle leakage ▶

Symptom. Early on, validation sets were drawn per-source; when mixture weights changed which sources existed, each arm was being validated on a subtly different distribution — laws fit on self-graded homework.

Fix. Froze a mixture-independent validation battery (fixed sets per stream, frozen tokenizer, frozen image tokenization) before block T1, versioned like code. Trivial in hindsight; the single most common silent killer of data-law projects, because the bug produces smoother, prettier curves, not uglier ones.

C4 · The exponent identifiability trap: α and β trade off against E ▶

Symptom. Bootstrap revealed huge covariance between E_s and β_s on streams where our sweep didn't reach low loss (video): the fit could explain the same data with "high floor, fast decay" or "low floor, slow decay" — wildly different frontier implications, identical fit quality.

Fix. Honesty over false precision: for those streams I reported the CI region and refused a point allocation, and bought identifiability where it was cheap — a few extra-long branches on small N to pin the D→∞ behavior. Where identifiability couldn't be bought, the law says "insufficient evidence" and the allocation fell back to conservative priors. A law that knows what it doesn't know is the difference between science and curve-fitting theater.

§9 · The final law, and how it was actually used

DELIVERED ARTIFACT (schematically):
  η*(N, w)              — μP base + patch-embed multiplier + mixture correction
  L_s(N, D, w)          — per-stream law with sparse interference terms
  D_eff = Σ q_i(N)·U_i·(1−r_i^e)/(1−r_i)   — quality & repetition, per family
  Eval_e ≈ σ_e(L_s(...))                    — fitted loss→eval links
  w*(C) ± CI            — compute-dependent optimal mixture REGION
USED FOR:
  · the frontier recipe (w*, anneal composition, N–D split) — signed off
    against the law's prediction, verified within CI on 9/11 clusters
  · quarterly "what-if" service: recipe counterfactuals answered from the
    fitted surface in minutes, with CIs
  · the investment case for CapForge: q_i(N)·U_i showed unique gold tokens
    as the binding constraint — the law literally priced a data engine
The narrative spine, in one breath: tune first (μP + WSD, or the law measures mistuning); fit the base law two independent ways and force them to agree; promote D from a count to a structured object (quality vector × repetition decay per family); go multimodal with per-stream losses and a sparse-interference joint form; validate by extrapolation and decision-rank, never by interpolation R²; and report regions, not points, wherever the data doesn't identify the answer.

10.4 Eval suite & infra facts (the follow-up layer)

  • Eval battery: 11 capability clusters built from ~30 public+internal evals — doc/OCR (DocVQA, ChartQA, InfoVQA), counting/pointing, spatial, dense description (judged), multi-image, long-video, text reasoning (held as guardrail), etc. Clusters, not single benchmarks, because single benchmarks are too noisy at proxy scale — cluster averaging cut eval σ roughly in half.
  • Noise floor: from the 150M 3-seed runs — σ ≈ 0.15–0.3 pts per cluster at proxy scale; every claimed effect had to clear ~2σ paired. That σ is also the Huber δ in the fit — one measurement, two uses.
  • Decontamination: image-space (perceptual hash + embedding-NN at cosine ≥ 0.95 against every eval image) before any captioning; text n-gram overlap as the second net. Run before block T1, versioned with the validation battery.
  • Turnaround as a managed metric: a 300M sweep point was a same-day result, 1B ~2 days, 3B ~1 week. I tracked fits-per-week as the project's own KPI — the science is bottlenecked by iteration rate, so I treated proxy throughput as a first-class deliverable, not a byproduct.
  • One infra sentence worth saying: "Every run carried a pinned recipe hash — source snapshot IDs, filter versions, tokenizer version, tiling policy — and the fitter refuses runs whose hashes don't resolve; that rule exists because a silent pipeline upgrade once cost us 25 sweep points."

10.5 Three sentences that carry the whole §10 in an interview

  1. "The ladder was five sizes, 150M to 8B at half-decade spacing, early-fusion decoder-only with a frozen SigLIP-style tower and a canonical 256-tokens-per-tile policy — frozen encoder and fixed tokenization because otherwise every data coefficient is confounded."
  2. "The whole 160-run matrix cost under 15% of the frontier run it de-risked — WSD branching is what made that ratio possible."
  3. "Our exponents came out Chinchilla-adjacent on text — α about 0.32 — but the image-conditioned stream was clearly more data-limited, α about 0.24, and that asymmetry is the quantitative reason the optimal mixture drifts toward dense visual data as compute grows."

§11 · Future-looking agenda (what I'd do next — the vision question)

Interviews end with "where does this go?" Have a ladder: near-term extensions of what worked, mid-term bets, and one horizon idea that connects to your own research line.

11.1 Near-term (next two quarters — direct extensions)

Each stated the way I'd write the internal one-pager: hypothesis → experiment → kill criterion → payoff.

N1 · Context-length-aware proxy rung

Hypothesis: long-context MM data (long-video, multi-image, long interleaved docs) has its own data exponent, and 8K-context proxies structurally under-value it — which is exactly why our two frontier misses were both long-context clusters.

Experiment: add a 32K rung at 1B (context extension on the existing trunk — cheap, reuses WSD infra); rerun the 12 probe mixtures at both contexts; fit L_s(N, D, w, ctx) with a ctx-dependent B-term for the long-context families. ~15 runs.

Kill criterion: if the 32K rung's mixture ranking matches 8K within CI, context isn't a law axis — close the workstream, document, move on.

Payoff if right: the first principled answer to "how much long-video should be in the mix," currently the least-defended number in every MM recipe I know of.

N2 · Resolution/token-budget law (pixels and text under one currency)

Hypothesis: the marginal value of the k-th image token follows a per-task-family saturating curve — OCR/doc saturates late (fine text needs pixels), natural-scene VQA saturates early — so a content-conditional tiling policy dominates any fixed policy at iso-FLOP.

Experiment: 3×4 grid — {64, 256, 576} tokens/tile × 4 task-family-enriched mixtures at 1B, fixed total token budget so image tokens displace text tokens (that's the real tradeoff). Fit L_s(tokens/image) per family; solve the joint allocation.

Kill criterion: if the fitted saturation points cluster within 2× of each other across families, content-conditional routing isn't worth its complexity — ship one global number instead.

Payoff if right: a single marginal-value currency across modalities — "this image's 5th tile is worth 300 caption tokens" — which is the allocation primitive everything else in MM data science lacks.

N3 · Law-guided online mixing (the curriculum is the law's derivative)

Hypothesis: since the fitted w* moves with compute (§5.3), the optimal schedule is approximately the path w*(C) traced during the run — static mixtures leave capability on the table, and the fitted surface already contains the curriculum for free.

Experiment: three arms at 1B, iso-FLOP: static w*(C_final), the law-implied path w*(C(t)), and a DoReMi-style online controller as the learned baseline. Guardrail instrumentation for path-dependence (does visiting web-heavy early poison anything the law, which is path-blind, can't see?).

Kill criterion: path arm must beat static by >2σ on the headroom-weighted aggregate and show no guardrail regression; otherwise static + anneal is already capturing the effect and the extra machinery isn't paying.

Payoff if right: unifies mixture, curriculum, and anneal into one object — the anneal stops being a separate trick and becomes the tail of the w*(C) path, which is intellectually the cleanest result the project could produce.

11.2 Mid-term bets (the proposals I'd write)

M1 · Scaling laws for the data engine itself

The gap: frontier spend is shifting from crawling to manufacturing data (CapForge was exactly this), yet there is no published law pricing data-production compute against training compute. Every lab is making this allocation by intuition.

The proposed law: capability = f(C_gen, C_verify, H_seed) — generation compute, verification compute, human seed-set size. From CapForge I already have three fitted fragments: the captioner-quality/cost knee (90% of value at 15% of cost, except OCR-dense), the value-of-verification curve (T1-vs-T2 tier gap), and q's dependence on human-edit rounds. The proposal stitches them into one surface with a designed grid over (generator size × verifier strictness × seed size).

The decision it feeds: "next $X of compute: train on it, or generate-and-verify with it?" My prior from the fragments: verification compute is currently the under-bought term — its marginal curve was the steepest of the three — and I'd stake the first sweep on confirming that.

Risk to name unprompted: the surface is generator-model-dependent, so it must be refit per model generation — the law's shelf-life is one model cycle, and the proposal budgets for that refit rather than pretending permanence.

M2 · Post-training scaling laws done honestly

Why loss breaks: RL moves the policy, not the likelihood surface — perplexity can be flat while pass-rate doubles. So the state variable must change: pass-rate-vs-compute curves on verifiable MM tasks (rendered charts with known answers, counting, OCR exact-match, geometry), which are smooth, cheap, and cheat-proof.

Design: a 3-axis grid — base-model PT gold-token dose (reusing T2 checkpoints!) × SFT data dose × RL compute — measuring pass-rate per capability. The T2 reuse is the trick: the pretraining sweep's checkpoints become the post-training sweep's independent variable for free, which is a ~10× cost saving over training fresh bases and the kind of asset-reuse argument that gets proposals funded.

Target result: the elicitation frontier — iso-capability curves in the (PT gold tokens, RL compute) plane. Its slope is the exchange rate the PT↔post budget debate has always lacked. Secondary prediction to test: the frontier is L-shaped for perception-bound capabilities (no amount of RL rescues missing perception) but shallow for format-bound ones — B3-P3's perception/expression split, promoted to a law.

Kill criterion: if pass-rate curves aren't stable across seeds at feasible RL budgets (RL variance is notorious), fall back to best-of-k likelihood-free probes as the state variable before giving up on the frontier concept.

M3 · Transfer-matrix laws (does transfer grow with scale?)

The question: we found sparse positive cross-terms (text-math data lowered chart-stream loss). Is that a fixed fact, or does cross-modal transfer strengthen with scale — models becoming increasingly modality-agnostic learners, the optimistic reading of Apple's early-fusion results?

Design: estimate the full family×stream transfer matrix Γ (γs,i for all off-diagonal pairs) at 1B and 3B with matched relative budgets; test H₀: Γ(3B) = Γ(1B) against ‖Γ‖ growing. Requires the group-lasso machinery from §7 plus ~30 targeted add-one-family runs — the most expensive of the three bets, and the most publishable.

Why it's load-bearing either way: if transfer grows, mixture optimization gets easier at the frontier (data becomes fungible; buy the cheapest capable tokens); if it doesn't, per-modality data supply is a permanent strategic constraint and data-engine investment (M1) becomes existential. Either answer reallocates real money — the definition of a well-posed bet.

11.3 Horizon (the idea I'd end the interview on)

Laws in representation space, not token space. Everything above measures data value through next-token loss — but token loss over-rewards surface redundancy and under-measures perceptual grounding. The natural next instrument: measure data value by how it moves latent predictive quality — e.g., loss of predicting frozen SSL/JEPA embeddings of future or masked visual content, which is precisely the setting of my LatentFusion proposal (LLM predicting in frozen video-JEPA space). If a latent-space law predicts downstream perception better than token-space laws do — my bet is it will, because it's denoised of surface entropy — then the whole science of MM data gets a better state variable, and questions like "what is a video token worth" finally get a principled answer. That's the through-line: today's laws are built on a proxy metric; the next generation should be built on representations.

The concrete first experiment (so it's a plan, not a slogan): take the existing T1/T3 checkpoints — again reusing the sweep as an asset — and evaluate each on two batteries: (a) standard per-stream token loss, (b) latent-prediction loss against frozen DINOv2/V-JEPA embeddings of held-out masked/future visual content. Fit the same law family on both state variables; the test is which one better predicts the frontier eval vector we already measured. Zero new training runs for the pilot — it's pure re-evaluation of ~160 existing checkpoints, roughly a two-week project for the strongest possible framing: "I can tell you today whether representation-space laws beat token-space laws, because the sweep already exists." If (b) wins, the follow-up is a small dedicated sweep where mixture weights are optimized directly against latent-prediction loss — data curation guided by representation quality end to end.

11.4 The 30-second "vision" answer, verbatim

"Three steps. Near-term: make context length and image-token budget explicit axes of the law, and let the fitted surface drive the curriculum online instead of a static mixture. Mid-term: two missing laws — one that prices data-manufacturing compute against training compute, because that's where frontier spend is moving, and one honest post-training law built on pass-rate curves rather than loss. Long-term: I think token-space loss is the wrong state variable for multimodal data science — I want laws fit in representation space, predicting frozen SSL embeddings, which connects to my own proposal on latent-space prediction. Better state variable, better science."

Cross-exam Q&A

"Why not just trust Chinchilla's exponents and skip T1?" ▶

Because exponents are distribution- and stack-dependent: our tokenizer, context length, MM interleaving, and data quality all differ from MassiveText, and E certainly differs per stream. T1 also serves a second role Chinchilla's numbers can't: it calibrates my pipeline's noise floor and extrapolation error, which set the CIs on everything downstream. You replicate the base law not to learn the answer but to certify the instrument.

"μP or careful sweeps — pick one, why?" ▶

μP as backbone, sweeps as audit. μP collapses the width dimension of tuning nearly for free, but its guarantees are asymptotic and architecture-conditional — our vision patch-embed path violated its assumptions and needed an empirical multiplier. So: adopt the parametrization, then spend a small fixed budget verifying transfer at three widths on your architecture. Trust, with a receipt.

"Your mixture law has ~100 parameters. Overfit much?" ▶

Three defenses, in order of importance: group-lasso killed most cross-terms (interference is sparse — that's an empirical finding, not an assumption); every headline claim is validated on held-out mixtures by extrapolation and rank agreement, not fit quality; and bootstrap-over-runs CIs accompany every coefficient — where the CI was wide (video stream), we said so and declined to allocate on it. The parameter count is honest because the validation is out-of-sample.

"How would this change for post-training / RL data?" ▶

The machinery transfers but two assumptions break: loss stops being the right state variable (RL moves policies, not likelihoods — the link-function trick needs replacing with something like pass-rate-vs-compute curves), and data value becomes strongly order-dependent (curriculum effects dominate at small post-training scale, violating the mixture-weight abstraction). My honest answer: post-training scaling laws are an open problem; I'd start from dose–response and paired ablations there, and be suspicious of anyone selling smooth laws for RLHF data today.

"What was the single most consequential technical choice?" ▶

Per-stream validation losses instead of a single MM loss. It looks like bookkeeping, but it's what made E identifiable per modality, made interference measurable as sparse cross-terms, made the loss→eval links stable, and made the compute-dependent mixture result visible at all. Most of the project's findings would have been invisible under a scalar loss — averaging is where multimodal signal goes to die.
